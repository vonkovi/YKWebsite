On latency

Latency is the one number users feel without being told. They can’t see your architecture or your test coverage, but they feel the quarter-second between asking for something and getting it — and they judge the whole system by it.

The trap is treating latency as an average. Averages hide the tail, and the tail is what people remember: the one request in fifty that hangs long enough to break their concentration. A system that is fast on average and occasionally awful simply feels awful.

What helps is treating delay as a material, not a defect. Some waiting is unavoidable; the question is whether it’s honest. A response that arrives instantly but blank is worse than one that shows its work. The eye forgives a system that acknowledges it heard you.

So the goal isn’t only to be fast. It’s to be predictable, to fail visibly, and to spend your speed where attention actually lives — the first interaction, the empty state, the moment someone is deciding whether to trust you.

Everything after that, they’ll wait for.
